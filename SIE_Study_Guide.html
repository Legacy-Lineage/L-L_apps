Market Structure & Participants
Primary/secondary markets, broker vs. dealer, market makers
▼

Primary Market

  • New securities issued for first time
  • Proceeds go to the issuer
  • IPOs and follow-on offerings
  • Regulated by Securities Act of 1933

Secondary Market

  • Investors trade among themselves
  • No proceeds to issuer
  • NYSE, Nasdaq, OTC markets
  • Regulated by Exchange Act of 1934

Market Tiers

  • Third Market: OTC trading of exchange-listed securities by non-member firms
  • Fourth Market: Direct institution-to-institution trading, no broker-dealer involved
  • Dark Pools: Private ATSs where large orders trade anonymously to reduce market impact

Broker vs. Dealer

Broker (Agent)

  • Acts on behalf of customer
  • Earns a commission
  • Does NOT take the other side of the trade

Dealer (Principal)

  • Trades from its own inventory
  • Earns a markup/markdown
  • Takes the other side of the trade
💡 Memory Hook

Bid = Buy (dealer buys) · Ask = Sell (dealer sells)
The spread (Ask − Bid) is the dealer's profit for providing liquidity.

Underwriting Types

TypeRisk BearerKey Detail

⚠️ Common Trap

Green shoe / overallotment option = underwriter sells up to 15% MORE shares, then covers in aftermarket (buys if price falls = stabilization; exercises option if price rises = new shares). This is legal stabilization, NOT manipulation.

The Federal Reserve & Monetary Policy
OMOs, rates, QE, dual mandate
▼

Fed Tools (Memorize These)

ToolExpansionary ActionContractionary Action
Open Market OpsBuy securities → inject reserves → rates ↓Sell securities → drain reserves → rates ↑
Discount RateLower rate → cheaper bank borrowingRaise rate → more expensive borrowing
Reserve RequirementLower req → more lending powerRaise req → less lending power (rarely used now)
IORB RateLower → banks lend moreRaise → banks hold more reserves (floor on fed funds)
💡 Key Rates

Federal Funds Rate = overnight interbank lending rate (market rate, Fed targets it)
Discount Rate = set directly by Fed, charged to banks at the discount window
Prime Rate = Fed Funds + 3% — what banks charge best corporate customers
Rule: Discount Rate > Fed Funds Rate > SOFR (roughly)

  • Dual Mandate: Maximum employment AND stable prices (low inflation ~2%)
  • FOMC: Meets 8×/year to set fed funds target; 7 governors + 5 regional bank presidents vote
  • Quantitative Easing (QE): Fed buys long-term securities when short-term rates are near zero — pushes down long-term rates
  • Since March 2020: Reserve requirements eliminated for all depository institutions

Interest Rate Theories

  • Pure Expectations: Long rates = expected future short rates only; no premium
  • Liquidity Preference: Long rates = expected short rates + liquidity premium (explains normal upward slope)
  • Market Segmentation: Different investors prefer different maturities; supply/demand in each segment sets rates
💡 Core Relationship — Never Forget

Interest rates ↑ → Bond prices ↓
Interest rates ↓ → Bond prices ↑
They move in opposite directions. Always. Without exception.

Money Market Instruments (all mature ≤ 1 year)

  • T-Bills: U.S. government, 4/13/26/52 weeks, issued at discount, zero coupon — risk-free benchmark
  • Commercial Paper: Unsecured, highly-rated corporations, up to 270 days, sold at discount
  • Banker's Acceptances: Short-term bank-guaranteed drafts, used in international trade
  • Repos (Repurchase Agreements): Collateralized overnight borrowing — sell securities + agree to repurchase next day
  • Eurodollars: USD deposits in foreign banks, outside U.S. regulation, no reserve requirements
  • Federal Funds: Overnight interbank loans of excess reserves

Equity Securities
Common, preferred, ADRs, rights, warrants
▼

Common Stock

  • Voting rights on corporate matters
  • Residual claimant (last in liquidation)
  • Dividends not guaranteed
  • Capital appreciation potential
  • Preemptive rights (buy new shares first)

Preferred Stock

  • Priority over common for dividends & liquidation
  • Usually NO voting rights
  • Fixed dividend rate
  • Callable — issuer can redeem at set price
  • Convertible — can convert to common shares

Types of Preferred Stock

  • Cumulative: Unpaid dividends accumulate (dividends in arrears) — must all be paid before common receives any dividend
  • Non-cumulative: Missed dividends are lost forever
  • Participating: Can earn extra dividends beyond stated rate if company does well
  • Convertible: Holder can convert to common stock at a set ratio
  • Callable: Issuer can redeem at call price — benefits issuer, creates reinvestment risk for holder

Key Concepts

  • Stock Split (2-for-1): Shares double, price halves — market cap unchanged
  • Reverse Split (1-for-10): Shares reduce, price rises — often done to meet minimum listing requirements
  • Treasury Shares: Shares repurchased by issuer — no dividends, no voting rights
  • ADRs: USD-denominated certificates representing foreign company shares — trade on U.S. exchanges
  • Rights: Short-term (weeks), below-market subscription price to buy new shares — preemptive rights protection
  • Warrants: Long-term (years), issued by company, exercise creates new shares
Key Formulas
Market Cap = Share Price × Shares Outstanding
Book Value per Share = Total Shareholders' Equity ÷ Shares Outstanding
P/E Ratio = Price per Share ÷ Earnings per Share (EPS)
Dividend Yield = Annual Dividend per Share ÷ Current Price
EPS = Net Income ÷ Shares Outstanding
Debt Securities
Bonds, yields, duration, Treasuries, munis, corporates, MBS
▼

Yield Calculations

Bond Yield Formulas
Current Yield = Annual Coupon ÷ Current Market Price
YTM = Total return if held to maturity (accounts for capital gain/loss + all coupons)
YTC = Total return if called at first call date and call price
Nominal Yield (Coupon Rate) = Annual Coupon ÷ Par Value

If bond trades at DISCOUNT: Nominal < Current < YTM
If bond trades at PREMIUM: Nominal > Current > YTM
Always compute "yield to worst" = lower of YTM or YTC

Duration & Convexity

  • Macaulay Duration: Weighted average time to receive all cash flows — measured in years
  • Modified Duration: % price change for 1% (100 bps) change in yield. Duration 5 → price changes ~5% per 1% rate move
  • Rules: Higher coupon → lower duration. Longer maturity → higher duration. Zero-coupon has highest duration for its maturity
  • Convexity: The curvature; positive convexity means price rises more than duration predicts when rates fall (beneficial)
  • Negative Convexity: Callable bonds — price appreciation is capped because issuer calls bond as rates fall

💡 Municipal Bond Tax Rule

Municipal bond interest is generally federal income tax exempt. If you live in the same state as the issuer, it may also be exempt from state and local taxes (triple tax-exempt). This is why munis offer lower coupon rates — the after-tax yield is often comparable to higher-rate taxable bonds.

Options
Calls, puts, strategies, Greeks, break-even calculations
▼

Call Options

  • Right to BUY at strike price
  • Buyer: bullish (wants price to rise)
  • Seller: neutral/bearish
  • Max gain buyer: unlimited
  • Max loss buyer: premium paid
  • Max gain seller: premium received
  • Max loss seller: unlimited (naked)

Put Options

  • Right to SELL at strike price
  • Buyer: bearish (wants price to fall)
  • Seller: bullish/neutral
  • Max gain buyer: strike − premium
  • Max loss buyer: premium paid
  • Max gain seller: premium received
  • Max loss seller: strike − premium (stock → $0)
Break-Even Calculations (Memorize)
Long Call Break-Even = Strike Price + Premium
Long Put Break-Even = Strike Price − Premium
Short Call Break-Even = Strike Price + Premium
Short Put Break-Even = Strike Price − Premium

Option Premium = Intrinsic Value + Time Value
Intrinsic Value (Call) = Stock Price − Strike Price (if positive; else $0)
Intrinsic Value (Put) = Strike Price − Stock Price (if positive; else $0)

The Greeks (Know These Definitions)

  • Delta: $ change in option price per $1 change in stock. Calls: 0 to +1. Puts: -1 to 0. ATM ≈ ±0.50
  • Gamma: Rate of change in delta per $1 stock move — highest for near-expiration ATM options
  • Theta: Time decay — option loses value daily as expiration approaches; accelerates near expiry
  • Vega: Change in option price per 1% change in implied volatility — long options benefit from rising vol
  • Rho: Sensitivity to interest rate changes — least important Greek on SIE
⚠️ Naked Call = Unlimited Risk

A naked (uncovered) call writer has no cap on losses because the stock can rise indefinitely. This is the highest-risk option position. A covered call writer owns the underlying stock, capping risk at the stock's decline to zero minus premium received.

1 equity option contract = 100 shares of underlying stock. Premium quoted per share; multiply × 100 for total cost.

ETF vs. Mutual Fund

ETFs

  • Trade on exchange throughout day at market price
  • Price ≠ NAV (can trade at premium or discount)
  • In-kind creation/redemption mechanism
  • Generally more tax-efficient
  • May have bid-ask spread transaction cost

Open-End Mutual Funds

  • Priced once daily at NAV after close
  • Always transact at NAV (± load)
  • Continuously issue/redeem shares
  • May distribute capital gains annually
  • No secondary market trading
  • Closed-End Fund: Fixed shares issued at IPO, then trade on exchange — price can diverge significantly from NAV (premium or discount)
  • 12b-1 Fee: Annual fee charged to fund assets for distribution/marketing; capped at 1%; ≤0.25% for "no-load" designation
  • Expense Ratio: Annual operating costs ÷ average net assets; paid daily from fund assets, not separately invoiced
  • Turnover Rate: High turnover → more realized capital gains → less tax-efficient
Annuities & REITs
Fixed vs. variable, M&E charge, REITs 90% rule
▼

Fixed Annuity

  • Guaranteed interest rate
  • Insurance company bears investment risk
  • No market risk to annuitant
  • Sold by insurance agents
  • NOT a security (generally)

Variable Annuity

  • Return tied to subaccount performance
  • Annuitant bears investment risk
  • IS a security — requires securities license
  • M&E charge: ~1–1.5% annually
  • Surrender charges: typically 6–10 years

Annuity Phases

  • Accumulation Phase: Contributions grow tax-deferred; no current tax on earnings
  • Annuitization / Payout Phase: Converts value to income stream; generally irrevocable
  • Life Only: Highest payment; stops at death — nothing to heirs
  • Life with Period Certain: Guaranteed minimum period (e.g., 20 years); heirs get remainder if death is early
  • Joint & Survivor: Continues for spouse's life — lower payment
⚠️ Tax Treatment of Withdrawals

Non-qualified annuity withdrawals are taxed LIFO — earnings come out first and are taxed as ordinary income. A 10% penalty applies to withdrawals before age 59½. Contributions (cost basis) come out last, tax-free.

REITs

  • Must distribute ≥ 90% of taxable income to shareholders (no corporate income tax on distributed amounts)
  • Equity REIT: Owns and operates properties; income from rent
  • Mortgage REIT: Invests in mortgage loans/MBS; income from interest; sensitive to rate changes
  • Publicly traded REITs provide real estate exposure with stock-like liquidity
  • REIT dividends are generally taxed as ordinary income (not qualified dividend rate)
Municipal Securities
GO vs. revenue bonds, tax treatment, MSRB, tax-equivalent yield
▼
🔥 High-Frequency Muni Topics
GO vs. Revenue bond backing Triple tax-exempt Tax-equivalent yield formula MSRB jurisdiction Serial bond structure

General Obligation (GO) Bonds

  • Backed by full faith, credit, and taxing power of issuer
  • Voter approval usually required
  • Considered safer — issuer can raise taxes
  • Lower yields than comparable revenue bonds
  • Examples: schools, roads, general infrastructure

Revenue Bonds

  • Backed by revenues of a specific project only
  • No access to general tax base
  • Higher yields to compensate for higher risk
  • No voter approval required (typically)
  • Examples: toll roads, airports, hospitals, water utilities
💡 Memory Hook

GO = Government-backed taxing power (safer, lower yield)
Revenue = Revenue from the project only (riskier, higher yield)
If the toll bridge earns no revenue, revenue bondholders have no recourse to the city's general funds.

Tax Treatment

  • Federal income tax: Muni interest is generally EXEMPT
  • State/local tax: Exempt if investor lives in the issuing state → "double tax-exempt"
  • All three levels exempt: "Triple tax-exempt" — federal + state + local
  • AMT (Alternative Minimum Tax): Some "private activity bonds" (stadiums, airports) may be subject to AMT even though they're technically munis
  • Capital gains: Always taxable, even on municipal bonds
Tax-Equivalent Yield Formula (TEY)
TEY = Muni Yield ÷ (1 − Marginal Tax Rate)

Example: Muni yields 4%; investor in 35% tax bracket
TEY = 4% ÷ (1 − 0.35) = 4% ÷ 0.65 = 6.15%

Interpretation: The investor would need a 6.15% taxable yield to equal the 4% tax-free muni yield.
If taxable bonds yield less than 6.15%, the muni is the better after-tax choice.

MSRB — Municipal Securities Rulemaking Board

  • Jurisdiction: Broker-dealers and bank dealers that underwrite, trade, and sell municipal securities
  • Does NOT regulate: Municipal issuers themselves — the MSRB has no authority over cities, states, or school districts
  • Key function: Writes rules for muni dealers; maintains EMMA (Electronic Municipal Market Access) — public disclosure database
  • Enforcement: MSRB writes rules; SEC and FINRA enforce them against broker-dealers; bank regulators enforce for bank dealers
  • Series 52: Municipal Securities Representative exam (required for muni dealer reps)
  • Series 53: Municipal Securities Principal exam

Municipal Bond Structures

  • Serial Bonds: Portions of the issue mature at different dates — like a mortgage amortization schedule; most common muni structure
  • Term Bonds: All bonds in the issue mature on the same date; often paired with a sinking fund
  • Variable Rate Demand Obligations (VRDOs): Long-term bonds with short-term interest rate resets; investor has right to put back to issuer
⚠️ Common Trap: Not All Munis Are Tax-Free

While most muni interest is federally tax-exempt, capital gains on muni bonds are fully taxable. Also, private activity bonds may trigger AMT. And muni bond interest earned in a tax-advantaged account (IRA, 401k) loses its tax-exempt advantage — you'd be paying tax on withdrawal anyway, so the lower muni yield may not be worthwhile in a tax-deferred account.

💡 Systematic vs. Unsystematic Risk

Systematic (Market) Risk: Affects all securities; cannot be diversified away. Beta measures systematic risk. Examples: recessions, interest rate changes, inflation.

Unsystematic (Specific) Risk: Affects only a company or industry; CAN be eliminated through diversification. Examples: management scandal, product recall, industry disruption.

Diversification eliminates unsystematic risk. It cannot eliminate systematic risk. This distinction is a frequent SIE test question.

Time Qualifiers

  • Day Order: Expires at end of trading day — default for most orders
  • GTC (Good Till Cancelled): Active until executed or customer cancels; broker sets internal limits
  • IOC (Immediate or Cancel): Fill what you can immediately; cancel the rest
  • FOK (Fill or Kill): Fill entire order immediately or cancel entirely — no partial fills
  • AON (All or None): Fill entire amount or don't fill — not necessarily immediate
  • Not Held: Gives broker/trader discretion over timing and price for best execution

Margin Math — Must Know
Equity = Market Value − Debit Balance
Equity % = Equity ÷ Market Value

Margin Call Trigger: MV − Debit = 0.25 × MV → MV = Debit ÷ 0.75

Example: Debit = $9,000 → MV = $9,000 ÷ 0.75 = $12,000 triggers maintenance call

Buying Power = SMA × 2 (each $1 SMA supports $2 of new purchases at 50%)

Short Margin Equity = Credit Balance − Short Market Value
  • SMA (Special Memorandum Account): Accumulated buying power from excess equity — can be withdrawn as cash or used for new purchases
  • Restricted Account: Equity below 50% Reg T but above 25% maintenance — cannot make new purchases; must sell to release funds
  • Pattern Day Trader (PDT): ≥4 day trades in 5 business days in a margin account (if >6% of total trades) → requires $25,000 minimum equity
  • Loan Value: Maximum amount broker will lend = 50% of marginable market value
⚠️ Meeting a Margin Call

Options: (1) Deposit cash, (2) Deposit marginable securities, (3) Sell securities in the account. Brokers may liquidate positions WITHOUT notice if a margin call is not met promptly.

Short Selling & Regulation SHO

  • Locate Requirement: Before accepting a short sale, broker must have reasonable grounds to believe shares can be borrowed
  • Close-Out Requirement: Fails to deliver must be closed out by end of settlement day +3
  • Threshold Securities: Fails ≥10,000 shares + ≥0.5% of float for 5 consecutive days → enhanced close-out requirements
  • Rule 201 (Alt Uptick Rule): Circuit breaker — if stock falls 10%+ from prior close, short sales restricted to above current best bid for that day and next
  • Short Squeeze: Rising prices → forced covering → more buying → price rises further (self-reinforcing)
  • Dividend obligation: Short seller must pay "payment in lieu of dividends" to lender when stock pays dividends

Critical Exemptions from Registration

Additional Key Regulations

  • Blue Sky Laws: State-level securities regulations — require registration of securities offerings and broker-dealers in each state, unless a federal exemption applies. NSMIA preempted state registration for "covered securities" (exchange-listed stocks, Reg D Rule 506 offerings, investment company securities). States retain anti-fraud authority.
  • Regulation S-P: SEC rule requiring broker-dealers and investment advisers to adopt policies protecting the privacy of customer financial information. Requires annual privacy notices to customers; customers can opt out of information sharing with unaffiliated third parties.
  • USA PATRIOT Act: Post-9/11 law that significantly strengthened AML/BSA requirements. Key provision: requires financial institutions to implement Customer Identification Programs (CIP) to verify identity of customers opening new accounts. Enhanced due diligence for high-risk customers and jurisdictions.
  • Customer Identification Program (CIP): Under PATRIOT Act, firms must verify: name, date of birth, address, and ID number (SSN for U.S. persons; passport/alien ID for foreign persons) for all new account holders.
  • Regulation S-ID (Red Flags Rule): Requires firms to implement written identity theft prevention programs to detect, prevent, and mitigate identity theft in customer accounts.
  • Rule 506(b) — Reg D: Up to 35 non-accredited + unlimited accredited investors; no general solicitation; restricted securities
  • Rule 506(c) — Reg D: General solicitation permitted; ALL investors must be accredited and verified
  • Rule 144A: Resale of restricted securities to QIBs (institutions with $100M+ in securities); no holding period between QIBs
  • Rule 144: Resale of restricted/control securities by affiliates — holding periods, volume limits, manner of sale requirements
  • Reg A+ Tier 1: Up to $20M in 12 months; simplified disclosure
  • Reg A+ Tier 2: Up to $75M in 12 months; offering circular (not full S-1)
  • Reg CF: Crowdfunding up to $5M from general public including non-accredited investors; must use registered portal
  • Section 3(a)(11) / Rule 147: Intrastate exemption — offer and sale entirely within one state
💡 Howey Test — What Makes Something a Security

SEC v. W.J. Howey Co. (1946): An investment contract is a security if there is: (1) Investment of money + (2) In a common enterprise + (3) Expectation of profits + (4) Derived from the efforts of others. Courts apply this to crypto, LLCs, citrus groves, and other novel arrangements.

💡 FINRA 5% Markup Policy

A guideline, not an absolute rule. Markups, markdowns, and commissions on OTC transactions should not exceed 5%. Factors: security type, price, availability, firm costs. Some low-priced/illiquid securities are held to a tighter standard. Significantly above 5% = potential unfair dealing violation.

Three Stages of Money Laundering

1. Placement

  • Introducing dirty money into financial system
  • Cash deposits, smurfing, casino exchanges

2. Layering

  • Obscuring the trail
  • Wire transfers, shell companies, currency conversion

3. Integration

  • Funds re-enter legitimate economy
  • Real estate purchases, business investments

Red Flags for SAR Filing

  • Structuring/Smurfing: Breaking deposits into sub-$10K amounts to avoid CTR — illegal REGARDLESS of whether funds are clean
  • Transactions inconsistent with customer's stated business or financial profile
  • Customer unusually secretive about the source of funds or purpose of transactions
  • Requests to bypass normal documentation procedures
  • Large, unexplained wire transfers to/from high-risk jurisdictions
⚠️ SAR Tipping-Off Prohibition

Once a SAR is filed, the firm cannot inform the subject of the report. Tipping off the subject of a SAR is itself a federal crime. The SAR process is confidential.

Suitability, Reg BI & SIPC
Three suitability prongs, best interest obligations, SIPC coverage
▼

FINRA Rule 2111 — Suitability (Three Prongs)

  • Reasonable Basis Suitability: The recommendation must be suitable for at least some investors based on thorough analysis
  • Customer-Specific Suitability: Must be suitable for THIS particular customer's full investment profile
  • Quantitative Suitability (Anti-Churning): The overall number of recommended transactions must not be excessive for the customer's profile

Regulation Best Interest (Reg BI) — Four Obligations

  • Disclosure: Before or at the time of recommendation; must disclose capacity (broker vs. adviser), fees, conflicts
  • Care: Exercise reasonable diligence, care, skill — understand risks/rewards; have a reasonable basis that it's in the customer's best interest
  • Conflict of Interest: Establish, maintain, and enforce policies to identify and disclose (or eliminate) material conflicts
  • Compliance: Maintain written policies and procedures reasonably designed to achieve compliance

Reg BI applies to retail customers only. It goes further than suitability — broker may not put their financial interest ahead of customer's. Form CRS must be delivered at or before start of relationship.

SIPC Coverage

$500K
Maximum per customer at failed broker-dealer
$250K
Sub-limit for cash claims only
  • SIPC covers: Stocks, bonds, mutual fund shares, and cash held at a failed broker-dealer
  • SIPC does NOT cover: Market losses, investment fraud losses, commodities futures, fixed annuities, currencies, or investment contracts
  • SIPC is not government-funded — it is a nonprofit membership corporation funded by member broker-dealers
⚠️ SIPC Is Not FDIC

SIPC does NOT protect against investment losses. If your broker fails and your securities are missing — SIPC replaces them up to limits. If the market tanks and your portfolio loses value — SIPC does nothing. This is the most common SIPC misconception on the exam.

Most Common Exam Traps

Trap: SIPC vs. FDIC
▼

SIPC covers securities and cash at a failed broker-dealer. It does NOT cover market losses, fraud, or investment failure. FDIC covers deposits at failed banks. If a question says "SIPC protects against investment losses" — that's WRONG.

Trap: Stop Order ≠ Price Guarantee
▼

A stop order converts to a market order when triggered. In fast markets, you can be filled significantly worse than the stop price. Only a stop-limit order provides a price guarantee — but then it may not execute at all if the price gaps through the limit.

Trap: Reg T is Initial, Not Ongoing
▼

Regulation T (50%) applies at the time of purchase. After purchase, the ongoing requirement drops to the FINRA maintenance margin (25% long / 30% short). A margin call is triggered when equity falls below maintenance — NOT when it falls below 50%.

Trap: Discount Rate vs. Fed Funds Rate
▼

The federal funds rate is a market rate — the Fed targets it through open market operations but doesn't directly set it. The discount rate is directly set by the Fed and is charged to banks borrowing at the discount window. The discount rate is typically higher than the fed funds rate to discourage overuse of the discount window.

Trap: 1933 Act vs. 1934 Act
▼

1933 Act = primary market, new issuances, prospectus, registration. Think "truth about new securities." 1934 Act = secondary market, created the SEC, broker-dealer regulation, Rule 10b-5 fraud, Reg FD, Section 16. Think "ongoing market regulation and the SEC itself."

Trap: Structuring Is Always Illegal
▼

Structuring (breaking transactions into sub-$10K pieces to avoid CTR filing) is a federal crime under the Bank Secrecy Act — even if the underlying funds are completely legitimate. The act of deliberately structuring to avoid reporting is the crime, regardless of the source of funds.
